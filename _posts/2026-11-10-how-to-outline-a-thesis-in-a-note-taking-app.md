In an outliner such as Quillmark, you fold, drag and zoom branches to restructure, type `[[` to link each claim to its sources, and let backlinks show you which sources support which chapter. ## How do I go from reading notes to a chapter outline?

Plan the chapters first, then fill them. The steps below combine a standard thesis structure with a topic-sentence method for a single chapter.

### 1. Create a skeleton from your department's structure

Most empirical theses run introduction, literature review, methodology, results, and discussion and conclusion. [CASRAI's thesis structure guide](https://casrai.org/guides/thesis-structure-and-example-outline) describes the literature review as "organized thematically or conceptually, not as a list of summarized papers", and notes that humanities dissertations often use "a connected sequence of thematic chapters" instead. Your department's template decides which pattern applies.

In your app, make one top-level project note called "Thesis" with one branch per chapter and 3 to 6 empty section branches under each. Fold everything except the chapter you are working on.

To calibrate, skim a few dissertations from your own department. The [UNC Writing Center](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/dissertation-strategies) suggests reverse-outlining a few chapters to see "if there's a pattern of typical components and how information is sequenced." In an outliner, that means pasting each heading into a scratch note and indenting it by level.

### 2. Collect reading notes in one place

Historian Ann Daly's [chapter-writing method](https://thepanorama.shear.org/2026/06/03/dissertation-chapter) starts by collating all notes for a chapter into one notebook or file. In Quillmark, do this by giving every paper its own note and keeping a consistent shape:

- Citation line (author, year, title)
- Two or three sentences on what the source argues
- Quotes you may use, each as its own bullet with a page number
- A "Used for" bullet naming the chapter or section it supports

Capture fast and tidy later. The home page says you can [capture an idea in two keystrokes](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/) and fold it into a project later.

### 3. Write a one-paragraph purpose for each chapter

Under each chapter branch, write what the chapter argues in a short paragraph. Daly's test in [her chapter method](https://thepanorama.shear.org/2026/06/03/dissertation-chapter): "If you can't articulate the chapter's purpose in 200 words, then writing 10,000 words is going to result in a sprawling monster." Her next step is a road map of 500 to 800 words that she describes as an exercise "and not a piece of writing that others will read." Keep it as the first child of the chapter branch and fold it away.

### 4. Turn the chapter into topic sentences

Under the road map, add one bullet per paragraph, each holding a single claim. [Daly's rule](https://thepanorama.shear.org/2026/06/03/dissertation-chapter) is "you get one point per paragraph," and her arithmetic is that a 10,000-word chapter at 250 to 300 words per paragraph needs 33 to 40 paragraphs. Use that number as a sanity check: if your outline has 12 bullets, it is too thin; if it has 90, it is probably two chapters.

Now use the outliner's strength. Drag bullets up and down until the argument flows, indent related claims into sections, and zoom into one section at a time. Quillmark's own post on [outlining versus linear notes](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/blog/2026/09/14/outlining-vs-linear-notes/) puts the reason plainly: outlines "let you move ideas without rewriting them."

### 5. Hang the evidence under each claim

Daly's fifth step is to find the sources for each paragraph and put them under the topic sentence, with citations so you can find the originals. She adds a useful test: "If you can't find evidence for a paragraph, maybe the point doesn't belong, or you need to go back and adjust your claims." In the outline, mark unsupported claims with a tag or a "?" and decide at the next review whether to cut or research them.

### 6. Decide how detailed to go

Grad students often get stuck here. One [Outliner Software forum post](https://www.outlinersoftware.com/topics/viewt/12712) describes the trade-off: "A very short outline gives me freedom while drafting, but I can lose track of the larger structure," while a detailed one "can start to feel like I'm writing the paper twice." A workable middle is topic sentences plus linked sources, with no full paragraphs. Once a section has all its claims and evidence, start drafting it.

The UNC handout adds that you can [write chapters in "the wrong order"](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/dissertation-strategies) if that gets you moving. With one tree per chapter, you can start with whichever is easiest.

## How do I link sources to outline points?

Use one note per source and link to it from every claim that relies on it.

1. In the claim bullet, type `[[` and pick the source note. [Quillmark links both ways](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/), so you do not set up the reverse link yourself.
2. Open any source note and look at its backlinks. [Every note shows what links to it](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/features/), so you can see which chapters lean on that paper. A source with no backlinks has not earned its place yet. A source linked from four chapters is probably central to your argument.
3. When a single bullet from your reading notes becomes an idea you will develop (a concept, a case, a dataset), use the shortcut that [turns a bullet into its own note](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/features/) and link it like any other note.
4. When you cannot remember which paper made a point, use semantic search. It [searches by meaning](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/features/), so a phrase like "that idea about onboarding emails" can find a note without those exact words. Semantic search is part of [Pro](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/pricing/), which students get free.

One limit to know about: Quillmark does not offer block references, so you link whole notes rather than a single quoted line. The [comparison with Logseq](https://wespreadjam.github.io/jam-qa-md/blog/2026/11/10/quillmark-vs-logseq/) says reuse is done by turning a bullet into its own note and linking it with `[[`. If you want to cite a single block from many places, Logseq lets you type `((` to search for blocks, and it is free open-source software. The cost is its mobile apps and sync, covered in that comparison.

Keep full citation details in each source note's first line, and keep your reference manager as the source of truth for formatting.

## How does this change for a master's thesis, a PhD, or a three-paper dissertation?

- **Master's thesis.** CASRAI says the [chapter logic is the same, but shorter](https://casrai.org/guides/thesis-structure-and-example-outline), with a narrower literature review. Use the same tree with fewer sections and fewer sources per claim.
- **PhD dissertation.** Expect longer chapters. CASRAI's illustrative composite gives indicative ranges of 8,000 to 12,000 words for a literature review and 5,000 to 9,000 for results, and says they are "indicative ranges rather than a required template." Divide by 250 to 300 words per paragraph to get a rough bullet count per chapter.
- **Paper-based dissertation.** In this format [each paper becomes its own self-contained chapter](https://casrai.org/guides/thesis-structure-and-example-outline), connected by an introduction and a concluding synthesis. Make each paper a branch with its own tree, and put the linking argument in a separate "Synthesis" branch that links to all of them.

Terminology also differs: in the US and Canada "dissertation" usually means doctoral work and "thesis" a master's, and CASRAI notes this is typically reversed in the UK, Ireland, Australia and much of Europe. Check your graduate school's formatting rules, which CASRAI calls a common cause of delayed submission when ignored.

## FAQ

**What is the best way to outline a thesis?**
Start from your department's chapter structure, write a short purpose for each chapter, then break each chapter into one-claim-per-paragraph topic sentences with sources underneath.

**How many bullets should a chapter outline have?**
Using [Daly's math](https://thepanorama.shear.org/2026/06/03/dissertation-chapter), a 10,000-word chapter at 250 to 300 words per paragraph is about 33 to 40 paragraphs, so about 33 to 40 topic-sentence bullets.

**Do I need to outline the whole thesis before writing?**
No. You can draft chapters in whatever order gets you moving, per the [UNC Writing Center](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/dissertation-strategies). Outline the chapter you are about to write in detail and keep the others at section level.
